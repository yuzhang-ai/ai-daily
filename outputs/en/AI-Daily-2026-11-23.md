### 1. Lean and Mathlib Community: Joint statement certifies the Lean proof of the Riemann Hypothesis as correct
- Sources: [Lean community statement](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/); [Alpöge announcement](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf); [MIT verification page](https://math.mit.edu/~drew/grh/); [Community repository](https://github.com/leanprover-community/riemann-hypothesis)
- Type: Research
- Rating: ★★★★★
- Summary: The Lean and Mathlib community issued a joint statement on November 21 certifying that the Lean proof of the Riemann Hypothesis published by Levent Alpöge on November 20 is correct in both statement and proof.
- Key figure: 1,700 万 lines | size of the Lean library in the community statement

**Why it matters:** The Riemann Hypothesis was proposed approximately 160 years ago and is one of the Millennium Prize Problems. Prior to this, although large-scale formal proofs had successively tackled the Four Color Theorem, the Feit-Thompson Theorem, and the Liquid Tensor Experiment in Lean, none of the verification targets had been the Riemann Hypothesis itself. On November 12, the Xena Project published a post setting out four criteria for "a proof has been checked in Lean." After Alpöge released the proof repository on November 20, the community completed verification based on these four criteria and issued a joint statement at 13:55 UTC on November 21 (21:55 Beijing time), co-endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative.

**Key points:**
1. The statement explicitly notes that the Lean repository released by Alpöge on November 20 contains approximately 17 million lines, with Mathlib as its only direct dependency; the community confirmed that its statement is taken from `RiemannHypothesis` in Mathlib, that the repository compiles, that the proven theorem aligns with the statement, and that the exported proof is also accepted by multiple proof checkers.
2. In an announcement on the same day, Alpöge stated that he discovered and formalized this proof "together with Claude." The community repository and the MIT verification page also list related theorems on the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions; the MIT page explicitly notes that the GRH here covers only Dirichlet L-functions, excluding Dedekind zeta, Hecke L-functions, and general automorphic L-functions.
3. In terms of community discussion, the Hacker News thread discussing the statement reached 1,209 points. Andrew Sutherland's page at MIT aggregated results from independent compilation, kernel replay, and comparator checks, with the header updated at 2026-11-22 16:20 UTC.

**What it means:** Along the evolutionary path of mathematical formal verification, what the Lean community certified this time is the Riemann Hypothesis itself, checked against an existing standard statement in Mathlib rather than an ad-hoc rewritten proposition; the author also explicitly attributed the discovery process to a collaboration with Claude in the first-hand announcement. Assessment: Moving forward, what is more critical is not re-announcing that it has "been proven," but whether the mathematical community can distill the new ideas in these 17 million lines of code into a readable argument; until then, this achievement is first and foremost a machine artifact that can be independently checked by kernels, rather than an academic paper in the traditional sense.

**Risks and open questions:** As of November 22 at 16:20 UTC, the MIT summary page still notes that some checks, such as Lean kernel replays on the exported files, have not yet concluded. Passing formal verification merely means that, under the kernels and Mathlib definitions used, the stated proposition can be logically derived from the three standard axioms; it does not equate to humans fully understanding all the new ideas in the argument. Alpöge himself also stated that he has not yet mastered large areas of mathematics surrounding the argument, which is why he is currently only publishing the Lean codebase. In addition, the specific division of labor between human and Claude remains a unilateral statement by the author; the joint statement also does not extend the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis beyond Dirichlet L-functions.

### 2. OpenAI: Chief Scientist publishes internal pre-training perplexity and proposes it as an industry safety ruler
- Sources: [Official blog](https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence); [Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=50601525)
- Type: Research
- Rating: ★★★★☆
- Summary: OpenAI Chief Scientist Jakub Pachocki published a post on November 22 disclosing the company's internal pre-training log-perplexity curves and advocating perplexity as a common metric for cross-lab comparisons and measuring safety cadence.
- Key figure: ~0.76 | The post states models began delivering breakthrough mathematical results after reaching this log-perplexity

**Why it matters:** Treating training loss as a leading indicator of model capability evolution is not a novel concept; scaling laws and discussions around emergent abilities have relied on this for years. Prior to this, however, frontier labs rarely published their pre-training curves on internal primary evaluation sets over a timeline, and even more rarely proposed them as a shared ruler for measuring the "cadence" of industry safety; this is compounded by the dilemma that downstream evaluation benchmarks broadly face rapid saturation. Pachocki's post, dated November 22, pairs internal figures with a proposal for a cross-lab ceiling.

**Key points:**
1. On the company's current primary evaluation set, the post discloses that the log-perplexity of GPT-3.5 is approximately 0.99 and GPT-4 is approximately 0.91; it states that when loss drops below 0.8, models have already demonstrated "transformative" capabilities in domains such as cybersecurity and mathematics. A footnote specifically notes that these data come from an internal codebase held-out set continuously used since GPT-3.5, and is a fixed snapshot version.
2. The post notes that after reaching a log-perplexity of approximately 0.76, the model began delivering breakthrough results on open mathematical problems; the author expects that upon entering the ~0.68–0.7 window, capabilities not covered by today's benchmarks may also emerge. The author also proposes establishing a shared ceiling based on measurements like perplexity, which developers commit not to exceed until specific monitoring milestones (such as successor solutions to chain-of-thought monitoring) are reached.
3. The post also lists several limitations: evaluation sets and training data differ across labs, making direct cross-lab comparisons difficult; while chain-of-thought reasoning significantly improves model capabilities, it does not lower the perplexity achieved by the model; additionally, the intrinsic entropy of data is unknown, and the loss value theoretically cannot drop to 0. Regarding discussions that an "avalanche"-like breakthrough in pre-training might occur in 2027, the author attributes this in a footnote primarily to long-term research accumulation and compute investment, with automated means accounting for only a minor portion, while noting that recent models have already featured meaningful design contributions from AI.

**What it means:** On this trajectory of using loss to extrapolate capabilities, what is new today is that the chief scientist of a frontier lab brought out internal curves and attempted to turn them into a shared ruler for safety governance, rather than yet another downstream benchmark leaderboard sweep. Assessment: The figures listed in the post cannot be directly compared with training losses from other companies, and the author explicitly notes the incomparability between datasets; its core utility lies in converting "whether training should continue to be pushed forward" into concrete thresholds that can be examined. In addition, the post's extrapolation of pre-training acceleration in 2027 remains a forward-looking judgment rather than an already realized product reality.

**Risks and open questions:** The figures hold only on OpenAI's internal codebase held-out set, and external parties cannot independently reproduce them based on the same data. Capability changes brought by post-training methods such as chain-of-thought are not reflected on this perplexity curve. The shared ceiling currently remains merely a proposal, with no adoption or enforcement mechanism yet in the industry. The Hacker News thread reached 452 points.
